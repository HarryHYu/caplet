# What is investing?

## What you will learn

- Explain how setting money aside differs from buying something for a future benefit.
- Name common ways people invest.
- Connect possible gains, possible losses and the time money may be needed.

## Saving and investing

**Saving** means keeping money for later instead of spending it now. **Investing** means putting money into something with the aim of receiving income or an increase in value. An **asset** is something you own that has value. **Income** is money received, such as pay or a payment from an investment.

These ideas can overlap. Money in a bank account is an asset. It can also earn **interest**, which is money paid for the use of money. Bank deposits do not change in price like company shares. Their buying power can still fall when prices rise. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/how-to-invest/choose-your-investments))

The important question is what the money is meant to do. Keeping money for a known purchase is different from accepting an uncertain future value. Calling an activity “investing” does not make it suitable for every purpose.

## What people can own

Common investment types include cash, bonds, property and shares. A **bond** is a loan to a government or business under stated terms. A **share** is a small ownership part of a company. Property includes land and buildings. Each type can make or lose money in different ways. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/how-to-invest/choose-your-investments))

It helps to separate the thing owned from the way it earns money. A building is the asset. A payment for using that building is income. A higher sale value is a change in the asset's value. These are different parts of the same story.

An investment description becomes clearer when it answers three ordinary questions: What exists? Who owns it? Where would a payment come from? A familiar name alone does not answer those questions.

## Return and risk

**Return** is the gain or loss from an investment over a stated time. It can include income and a change in value. **Risk** is the chance that the result will be worse than expected, including losing money. A larger expected return usually involves more risk. An expected return is a possibility, not a promise. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/how-to-invest/develop-an-investing-plan))

A **fee** is a charge for a service. Fees reduce the amount left for the owner. Comparing only the final value with the starting amount can miss payments received or costs paid along the way.

Words also matter. “Could earn” is different from “will earn”. A calculation can show what happens under an assumption. It cannot prove that the assumption will happen.

## Why time matters

An **investment time frame** is how long someone expects to leave money invested. Money needed soon gives less time to respond to a fall in value. Access matters too: selling may take time or involve costs. These questions are part of Moneysmart's investing plan guidance. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/how-to-invest/develop-an-investing-plan))

A longer time frame does not erase uncertainty. A family may be able to delay one goal but need another payment on a fixed date. The same person can therefore have different needs for different amounts of money.

## Worked example

*These numbers are not real. They only show the idea.*

Mina buys a fictional asset and later sells it. This simplified example leaves out tax.

**Money received**

- Sale money: $1,100
- Income received while owning it: $100
- Total received: **$1,200**

**Money paid**

- Purchase price: $1,000
- Total fees: $50
- Total paid: **$1,050**

**Result**

- Total received less total paid: **$150**

The gain is $150, not just the difference between the purchase and sale prices. If the sale money were lower, the result would also be lower. Nothing here predicts a real investment's result.

## Things to think about

- When would the money be needed, and could that date change?
- Is the possible result stated before or after all costs?

## Check your understanding

1. What is an asset?
   - a) Anything someone hopes to earn
   - b) Something owned that has value
   - c) A guaranteed payment
2. True or false: Taking more risk guarantees a larger return.
3. Why does the date money is needed matter when thinking about investing?

## Answers

1. **b) Something owned that has value.** Ownership and value are the key ideas.
2. **False.** A higher possible return can come with a greater chance of loss.
3. **There may be little time to respond to a fall or delay in selling.** Timing affects whether money is available for its purpose.

## Glossary

- **Saving:** Keeping money for later.
- **Investing:** Using money with the aim of income or growth in value.
- **Asset:** Something owned that has value.
- **Income:** Money received.
- **Interest:** Money paid for the use of money.
- **Bond:** A loan to a government or business under stated terms.
- **Share:** A small ownership part of a company.
- **Return:** An investment's gain or loss over a stated time.
- **Risk:** The chance of a worse result, including loss.
- **Fee:** A charge for a service.
- **Investment time frame:** How long money is expected to remain invested.
