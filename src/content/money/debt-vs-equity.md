# Debt vs equity

## What you will learn

- Explain borrowing and ownership as different ways to fund a business.
- Compare payment commitments with sharing ownership.
- Identify what a funding example can and cannot show.

## Two different relationships

**Finance** means money used to support an activity, such as running a business. **Debt** is money owed to someone else. **Equity** is an ownership interest in a business. These are different relationships, even when both provide money for the same project.

A business receiving borrowed money has an agreement to repay it. A business receiving money in exchange for ownership has added an owner. To understand the arrangement, ask what the other person receives in return: a promise of repayment or part ownership.

The distinction matters before comparing dollar amounts. Money arriving in a business account does not explain whether it came from customers, lenders or owners.

## Debt: money owed

A **lender** provides money under a repayment agreement. **Interest** is the charge for using borrowed money over time. A **repayment** returns money under the agreement.

A **bond** is one form of borrowing by a company or government from investors. Moneysmart explains that bond investors lend money and receive interest under the bond's terms. It also identifies the risk that the borrower cannot pay. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/investments-paying-interest/bonds))

For a business, the payment commitment matters even when sales disappoint. A plan that assumes every month will be successful may leave important uncertainty hidden. The question is whether the business could meet the agreed payments under different conditions.

Debt does not, by itself, give the lender ordinary ownership shares. The lender's position comes from the lending agreement. Some financial products are more complex, but this lesson considers a simple loan only.

## Equity: sharing ownership

A **share** is a unit of ownership in a company. A **shareholder** owns shares. A **dividend** is a payment a company makes to shareholders from profits. Moneysmart explains that not every company earns profits or pays dividends, and that share values can fall. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/shares/choosing-shares-to-buy))

For a business receiving new equity money, there is another owner to consider. Existing owners may hold a smaller proportion after new shares are issued. **Dilution** means this reduction in an existing owner's percentage.

A smaller percentage is not the same as a known dollar loss. The whole business might become more valuable, less valuable or stay the same. A percentage tells how ownership is divided; it does not predict the future business value.

## Compare costs without calling either free

Interest is an obvious debt cost because it is a stated payment. Equity also has a cost to existing owners: they share ownership and possible future benefits.

A simple equity arrangement does not promise the same scheduled return of money as a simple loan. However, that does not make it free money. Decisions about ownership and payment rights need their own explanation.

From the investor's side, both arrangements involve uncertainty. A lender may not receive what is owed. A shareholder may lose value and receive no dividend. The label alone cannot establish whether a particular arrangement is suitable.

## Worked example

*These numbers are not real. They only show the idea.*

A fictional business needs $1,000. Compare two separate ways to obtain it. They are alternatives, not two transactions together.

**Debt alternative**

- Money borrowed: $1,000
- Invented agreed interest: $100
- Total repayment under this example: **$1,100**

**Equity alternative**

- Money paid for newly issued shares: $1,000
- Money received by the business: **$1,000**

In the equity alternative, the invented agreement gives the new owner one quarter of the company after the issue. It does not promise a $1,100 repayment. The existing owner keeps three quarters and shares future ownership benefits and risks.

## Things to think about

- Does the document promise repayment, grant ownership, or combine features?
- What happens if the business earns less than expected?
- Is an ownership percentage being confused with a guaranteed money return?

## Check your understanding

1. What does equity represent?
   - a) A guaranteed interest payment
   - b) An ownership interest
   - c) Every business bill
2. True or false: A company must always make a profit and pay a dividend.
3. What is one difference between the two funding alternatives in the example?

## Answers

1. **b) An ownership interest.** It describes part ownership in the business.
2. **False.** Profits and dividends are not guaranteed.
3. **The debt alternative has an agreed repayment; the equity alternative shares ownership.** Their obligations differ.

## Glossary

- **Finance:** Money supporting an activity.
- **Debt:** Money owed.
- **Equity:** An ownership interest.
- **Lender:** Someone providing money under a repayment agreement.
- **Interest:** A charge for borrowed money.
- **Repayment:** Money returned under an agreement.
- **Bond:** Borrowing by a company or government from investors.
- **Share:** A unit of company ownership.
- **Shareholder:** An owner of shares.
- **Dividend:** A payment from company profits to shareholders.
- **Dilution:** A reduction in an existing owner's percentage.
