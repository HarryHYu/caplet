# How interest works

## What you will learn

- Explain an interest rate.
- Compare simple and compound calculations.
- Explain why borrowed money can cost more than the amount received.

## A payment linked to money and time

**Interest** is a payment for using money over time. A saver may receive it from a bank. A borrower may pay it to a lender. An **interest rate** expresses that payment as a percentage of an amount for a stated period. A percentage means a number out of one hundred.

The period is part of the meaning. A rate described as yearly cannot be treated as a monthly rate. The amount used in the calculation matters too. A rate without an amount and a period is not enough information to calculate a dollar payment.

The **principal** is the starting amount saved or borrowed. The **balance** is the amount in an account, or still owed, at a particular time. These amounts may be equal at the beginning and different later.

## Simple and compound calculations

**Simple interest** is calculated on the starting principal. **Compound interest** includes interest already added to the balance when the next interest amount is calculated. Moneysmart explains this difference using savings. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/saving/compound-interest))

Imagine explaining the calculation without numbers. With simple interest, the same starting amount is used again if the rate and period stay the same. With compound interest, the calculation starts from the updated balance. This difference explains why two otherwise similar calculations can produce different totals.

Compounding needs a schedule. “Added yearly” and “added monthly” describe different schedules. The worked example below uses yearly additions only. It does not describe any real bank account.

## Read account conditions with the rate

A **savings account** is an account designed for money kept aside. Providers can offer additional interest when particular conditions are met. They may also use temporary rates or charge fees. A **fee** is a separate charge for a product or service. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/banking/savings-accounts))

This means an arithmetic result is only as useful as its assumptions. A classroom calculation that ignores fees cannot establish the actual amount someone would receive. A calculation that holds the rate constant does not show what happens after a temporary offer ends.

It is possible to understand the calculation without selecting an account. The skill is to identify the principal, rate, period, schedule and any amounts added or removed.

## Interest on borrowed money

A **loan** is money provided under an agreement to repay it. A **repayment** is money paid back under that agreement. Interest and fees can make the total amount repaid greater than the money originally borrowed. Moneysmart distinguishes the interest rate from other loan charges. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/loans/personal-loans))

A **debt** is an amount owed. When a debt balance changes, its interest calculation may change too. It is therefore misleading to assume that every repayment reduces the amount owed by the full payment: some money may cover interest or charges.

The same arithmetic idea has different meanings for different people. Interest received adds to a saver's money. Interest charged adds to a borrower's cost. A larger calculated amount is not automatically a better outcome.

## Worked example

*These numbers are not real. They only show the idea.*

Two imaginary calculations begin with $1,000. Both use a made-up yearly rate of ten per cent for two years. No money is added or removed, and there are no fees.

**Simple interest**

- Starting amount: $1,000
- First-year interest: $100
- Second-year interest: $100
- Final amount: **$1,200**

**Interest compounded yearly**

- Starting amount: $1,000
- First-year interest: $100
- Second-year interest on the new balance: $110
- Final amount: **$1,210**

The extra $10 comes from interest on interest already added. It is a result of these invented assumptions, not an available account rate or promised return.

## Things to think about

- What time period does a quoted rate describe?
- Does a calculation include fees or money taken out?
- Is the person receiving interest or paying it?

## Check your understanding

1. What does compound interest include?
   - a) Only account fees
   - b) Interest already added to the balance
   - c) Only the original principal forever
2. True or false: A yearly rate and a monthly rate mean the same thing.
3. Why might a borrower repay more than the original loan?

## Answers

1. **b) Interest already added to the balance.** It becomes part of the next calculation.
2. **False.** The period changes the meaning of the rate.
3. **Interest and fees add costs.** Repaying the original amount alone may not cover them.

## Glossary

- **Interest:** A payment for using money over time.
- **Interest rate:** Interest expressed as a percentage for a stated period.
- **Principal:** The starting amount saved or borrowed.
- **Balance:** The amount held or owed at a time.
- **Simple interest:** Interest on the starting principal.
- **Compound interest:** Interest that includes earlier interest added.
- **Savings account:** An account for money kept aside.
- **Fee:** A separate charge.
- **Loan:** Money provided under a repayment agreement.
- **Repayment:** Money paid back.
- **Debt:** An amount owed.
