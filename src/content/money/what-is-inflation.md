# What is inflation?

## What you will learn

- Distinguish one price change from a change in prices overall.
- Explain Australia's main measure of consumer price changes.
- Connect rising prices with the buying power of pay and saved money.

## Prices across many purchases

**Inflation** means an increase in the general level of prices for things households buy. **Goods** are physical items, such as food. **Services** are activities people pay for, such as a haircut. A rise in one price alone does not tell us what prices are doing overall. ([RBA](https://www.rba.gov.au/education/resources/explainers/inflation-and-its-measurement.html))

“Prices rose more slowly” and “prices fell” mean different things. Slower increases still leave prices higher. A fall in the general price level is called **deflation**. ([RBA](https://www.rba.gov.au/education/resources/explainers/inflation-and-its-measurement.html))

This distinction can make a news headline easier to read. The speed of change and the level reached are different pieces of information. A lower inflation number does not, by itself, mean the cost of yesterday's shopping has returned to an earlier level.

## Measuring a broad change

The **Consumer Price Index (CPI)** measures changes in prices of a selected collection of household goods and services. The Australian Bureau of Statistics, or ABS, is the government agency that produces Australia's CPI. Items that make up more household spending have more influence on the measure. ([RBA](https://www.rba.gov.au/education/resources/explainers/inflation-and-its-measurement.html))

A national measure does not describe every family's exact spending. One family may spend more on transport; another may spend more on care. If those prices change differently, their experience can differ even while both read the same headline.

For a fair comparison, it matters whether the quantities and items stayed the same. A larger shopping total could reflect higher prices, extra items, or both. That is why a price comparison and a comparison of total spending answer different questions.

## Pay and saved money

**Purchasing power** means how much goods and services an amount of money can buy. **Income** is money received, including pay. If prices rise faster than income, that income buys less. The dollar amount alone does not show this change. ([RBA](https://www.rba.gov.au/education/resources/explainers/australias-inflation-target.html))

**Savings** are money kept for later. **Interest** is money paid for the use of money. Even when savings earn interest, rising prices can reduce what the balance buys. Comparing changes over the same period helps distinguish more dollars from more purchasing power. ([RBA](https://www.rba.gov.au/education/resources/explainers/australias-inflation-target.html))

The question is practical: can the money still pay for the same things? A balance and a shopping list provide different information. Both are needed to answer that question clearly.

## Australia's inflation target

The Reserve Bank of Australia, or RBA, is Australia's central bank. It guides national monetary conditions. Its flexible inflation target is annual consumer price inflation between 2% and 3%, with policy set so inflation is expected to return to the midpoint. This target guides decisions; it is not a promise about every price. ([RBA](https://www.rba.gov.au/education/resources/explainers/australias-inflation-target.html))

## Worked example

*These numbers are not real. They only show the idea.*

A fictional family's weekly pay stays unchanged. It buys exactly the same food and transport in both periods. These figures are not Australian inflation data.

**Earlier week**

- Pay available: $1,000
- Food: $200
- Transport: $100
- Total selected costs: **$300**
- Money left after selected costs: **$700**

**Later week**

- Pay available: $1,000
- Food: $220
- Transport: $110
- Total selected costs: **$330**
- Money left after selected costs: **$670**

The same purchases need $30 more. Unchanged pay leaves $30 less for other purposes. This small list illustrates purchasing power; it cannot be used to calculate the national CPI.

The comparison holds pay and quantities fixed so that the price effect is visible. If either changed too, a full explanation would need those details. It would be misleading to attribute every change in money left to inflation alone.

## Things to think about

- Is a headline describing slower price growth or falling prices?
- Does a personal spending comparison use the same items and quantities?

## Check your understanding

1. What does purchasing power describe?
   - a) The number of bank accounts
   - b) What an amount of money can buy
   - c) A company's total sales
2. True or false: Lower inflation always means prices are falling.
3. Why might a family's price experience differ from the national CPI?

## Answers

1. **b) What an amount of money can buy.** It connects money with goods and services.
2. **False.** Prices can still rise, just more slowly.
3. **Families buy different mixes of goods and services.** The national measure is not each family's exact shopping list.

## Glossary

- **Inflation:** A rise in the general level of consumer prices.
- **Goods:** Physical items.
- **Services:** Activities people pay for.
- **Deflation:** A fall in the general price level.
- **Consumer Price Index (CPI):** A measure of household price changes.
- **Purchasing power:** What an amount of money can buy.
- **Income:** Money received.
- **Savings:** Money kept for later.
- **Interest:** Money paid for the use of money.
