# Exchange rates

## What you will learn

- Read a price comparing two forms of money.
- Describe factors that can change that price.
- Explain why changes matter for travel, businesses and money sent overseas.

## Two currencies, one comparison

A **currency** is a system of money used in a country or region. The **Australian dollar (AUD)** is Australia's currency. An **exchange rate** is the price of one currency expressed in another currency. A rate is incomplete unless the currencies and direction are clear. ([RBA](https://www.rba.gov.au/education/resources/explainers/exchange-rates-and-their-measurement.html))

For an AUD quote expressed as foreign currency per Australian dollar, a higher number means each Australian dollar buys more foreign currency. **Appreciation** means a currency rises in value relative to another. **Depreciation** means it falls. These are comparisons between currencies, not labels for whether everyone benefits. ([RBA](https://www.rba.gov.au/education/resources/explainers/exchange-rates-and-the-australian-economy.html))

“Dollar” can refer to different currencies. The currency label therefore matters just as much as the number. This lesson writes all money amounts in Australian dollars. The worked example compares two Australian-dollar costs for the same overseas purchase.

## Why rates change

Australia has a **floating exchange rate**, meaning market demand and supply generally determine its value. **Demand** means how much buyers want to purchase. **Supply** means how much sellers make available. ([RBA](https://www.rba.gov.au/education/resources/explainers/exchange-rates-and-their-measurement.html))

An **interest rate** is a percentage paid for borrowing or saving over a stated period. Differences between Australian and overseas interest rates can affect demand for Australian dollars. So can prices of Australia's **commodity exports**, raw materials or farm products sold overseas. Money moving between countries for investment also matters. ([RBA](https://www.rba.gov.au/education/resources/explainers/drivers-of-the-aud-exchange-rate.html))

An **investment** uses money with the aim of future income or growth. Changes in what investors expect can affect their willingness to hold a currency. Several influences can operate together. One piece of news does not supply a complete explanation or a certain forecast.

This is why a statement about what a factor “can” do is different from a prediction. A rate can reflect many buyers and sellers responding to different information at the same time.

## Travel and trade

**Imports** are goods and services bought from overseas. **Exports** are goods and services sold overseas. When the Australian dollar falls, an unchanged overseas price generally costs more Australian dollars. A higher dollar generally has the opposite effect. Australian travel abroad can therefore become more or less expensive. ([RBA](https://www.rba.gov.au/education/resources/explainers/exchange-rates-and-the-australian-economy.html))

A lower Australian dollar can also make Australian goods cheaper for overseas buyers. That can help exporters, while making imported materials more costly. A business can experience both effects. ([RBA](https://www.rba.gov.au/education/resources/explainers/exchange-rates-and-the-australian-economy.html))

The question “Is a higher dollar good?” has no single answer. The effect depends on which currency someone receives and which currency they need to spend.

## Worked example

*These numbers are not real. They only show the idea.*

A fictional overseas accommodation booking has the same foreign-currency price at two times. After conversion, the Australian-dollar cost differs. An unchanged transfer charge is included. A **fee** is a charge for a service.

**First quote**

- Accommodation converted to Australian dollars: $500
- Transfer fee: $20
- Total Australian-dollar cost: **$520**

**Second quote**

- Same accommodation converted to Australian dollars: $600
- Transfer fee: $20
- Total Australian-dollar cost: **$620**

**Difference**

- Extra Australian dollars required: **$100**

Here the same overseas purchase costs $100 more. The fee has not changed, so the difference comes from conversion. These invented quotes do not show any current market rate.

## Sending money and comparing quotes

An **international money transfer** sends money between countries, often with a currency conversion. To understand two quotes, the comparison needs the same sending amount or the same receiving amount. Otherwise, a different result may come from different starting assumptions.

There are two separate questions: how much leaves the sender, and how much reaches the recipient? A displayed rate alone is not the whole calculation when a fee is also included. The worked example separates the conversion amount and charge to make that distinction visible.

## Things to think about

- Which currency does each dollar amount describe?
- Are two quotes for the same overseas amount and the same time?

## Check your understanding

1. What does an exchange rate compare?
   - a) Two people's wages
   - b) The values of currencies
   - c) Two tax returns
2. True or false: A lower Australian dollar benefits every Australian household and business.
3. Why does a comparison need clear currency labels?

## Answers

1. **b) The values of currencies.** It expresses one currency's price in another.
2. **False.** Effects differ for overseas purchases, exports and other activities.
3. **Different currencies can use the word dollar.** A number without its currency and conversion direction can be misunderstood.

## Glossary

- **Currency:** A system of money used in a country or region.
- **Australian dollar (AUD):** Australia's currency.
- **Exchange rate:** One currency's price in another.
- **Appreciation:** A rise relative to another currency.
- **Depreciation:** A fall relative to another currency.
- **Floating exchange rate:** A currency value generally determined by demand and supply.
- **Demand:** How much buyers want to purchase.
- **Supply:** How much sellers make available.
- **Interest rate:** Interest expressed as a percentage over a stated period.
- **Commodity exports:** Raw materials or farm products sold overseas.
- **Investment:** Money used for possible future income or growth.
- **Imports:** Goods and services bought from overseas.
- **Exports:** Goods and services sold overseas.
- **Fee:** A service charge.
- **International money transfer:** Sending money between countries.
