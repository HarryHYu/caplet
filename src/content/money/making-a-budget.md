# Making a budget

## What you will learn

- Explain how a plan for money works.
- Separate essential spending from optional spending.
- Read a simple plan for one month.

## Give each amount a purpose

A **budget** is a plan for money coming in and going out during a chosen period. **Income** is money received, such as pay from work. **Expenses** are costs paid to meet needs or wants. A budget compares these amounts. It is a useful question on paper: does the planned spending fit the available money?

Moneysmart's budget guide brings together income, everyday costs, less frequent bills and money set aside. It also explains that a plan needs updating when circumstances change. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/budgeting/how-to-do-a-budget))

A plan is an estimate, not a promise. Writing a higher income number cannot create more work hours. Writing a lower food number cannot make a household need less food. A useful plan describes the situation honestly enough for someone to understand it.

## Needs and wants are about purpose

**Needs** are essential costs. **Wants** are optional costs that can often wait. Moneysmart uses these groups to help people understand spending. A record of purchases can reveal costs that are easy to forget, including cash purchases. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/budgeting/track-your-spending))

The same item can serve different purposes. A phone used to receive work messages may meet a need. An optional upgrade may meet a want. Labelling the whole purchase without asking its purpose can hide this difference.

Families also have different needs. Travel to school, language classes or a medical appointment can affect what is essential. This lesson does not decide those priorities for any household. The categories are tools for discussion, not a way to judge people.

## Separate steady costs from changing costs

A **fixed cost** stays the same during the period being planned. A **variable cost** changes with use, price or circumstances. A fixed phone plan and changing food spending can both be needs. A fixed entertainment subscription can be a want. Fixed does not mean essential, and changing does not mean optional.

An annual bill is not a monthly bill simply because a budget covers one month. One approach is to show money set aside for that future bill separately. The important point is that the same money cannot also be counted as available for other spending.

## Keep the time period consistent

A one-month budget needs one month of income and one month of costs. Mixing a weekly income with monthly expenses produces a misleading result. Labels such as “expected this month” and “already paid” make the figures easier to understand.

People with changing work hours may also face changing income. Moneysmart discusses planning for lower-income periods rather than assuming every period will be the same. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/budgeting/managing-on-a-casual-income))

For classroom practice, it is possible to write an expected plan and then describe what would change if fewer shifts were offered. This is a question about uncertainty. It is not a forecast of anyone's actual pay.

## Worked example

*These numbers are not real. They only show the idea.*

Ari makes a one-month plan using money available after any deductions from pay.

**Income**

- Money from work: $800
- Total income: **$800**

**Planned uses**

- Fixed phone cost: $40
- Changing food and transport costs: $360
- Optional activities: $100
- Money set aside for future costs: $200
- Total planned uses: **$700**

**Money not yet allocated**

- Income less planned uses: **$100**

The final amount has not yet been given a purpose. It is not a guarantee that every expected cost has been included. Ari can check the list against the month being planned.

## Things to think about

- Which costs in a sample plan depend on how much a service is used?
- Does the plan include future bills, or only bills arriving this month?
- How could a family discuss different needs without assuming everyone has the same priorities?

## Check your understanding

1. What does a budget compare?
   - a) Only prices in different shops
   - b) Income and planned uses of money
   - c) Only optional purchases
2. True or false: Every fixed cost is a need.
3. Why must the income and expenses cover the same period?

## Answers

1. **b) Income and planned uses of money.** This shows whether the plan fits the available amount.
2. **False.** A fixed cost may pay for something optional.
3. **To make a fair comparison.** Weekly and monthly figures describe different lengths of time.

## Glossary

- **Budget:** A plan for money received and used over a period.
- **Income:** Money received.
- **Expenses:** Costs of needs and wants.
- **Needs:** Essential costs.
- **Wants:** Optional costs that can often wait.
- **Fixed cost:** A cost that stays the same in the planned period.
- **Variable cost:** A cost that changes.
