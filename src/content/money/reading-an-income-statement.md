# Reading a basic income statement

## What you will learn

- Recognise the main lines in a simple business report.
- Calculate two different profit amounts.
- Explain why a profit figure is not the whole story.

## A report about a period

An **income statement**, also called a profit and loss statement, summarises what a business earns and the costs counted against those earnings during a period. The period might be a month or a year. The heading matters: a monthly result cannot be compared directly with a full year's result.

ASIC, the Australian Securities and Investments Commission, is the government regulator for companies and financial services. It identifies income statements as a source for examining whether a company makes profits or losses. It points readers to different reports for cash movements and borrowing. ([ASIC](https://www.asic.gov.au/regulatory-resources/financial-reporting-and-audit/users-of-financial-reports))

This lesson uses a simplified classroom statement. Real reports can contain more lines and explanations. The purpose here is to learn the sequence of the calculation.

## Start with sales and their direct cost

**Revenue** is money earned from sales in the period. It is not automatically the amount left for the owner. A shop must obtain the products it sells and meet other costs.

**Cost of goods sold** is the cost assigned to the goods sold during that period. It is different from the selling price. In a simple shop example, this can be the purchase cost of the items that customers bought.

**Gross profit** is revenue minus cost of goods sold. The word “gross” tells the reader that this calculation has not yet removed all the other business expenses. **Expenses** are costs counted in measuring the business result.

It is easy to count the same cost twice. If the product purchase cost is already included in cost of goods sold, subtracting that identical cost again as another expense would make the result wrong. Each line needs a clear label.

## Then include other costs

**Net profit** is the amount left after all expenses included in the statement are deducted from revenue. A negative result is a **loss**: expenses exceed revenue.

A simple statement can move from revenue, to gross profit, to net profit. Each stage answers a different question. Gross profit asks what remains after the goods sold are counted. Net profit asks what remains after the other included expenses are counted too.

The example below assumes there are no further costs beyond the listed items. This assumption is essential. Without it, the final amount could only be described as profit before the omitted costs.

## Worked example

*These numbers are not real. They only show the idea.*

A classroom shop sells all the notebooks it bought for a one-month activity. Assume the listed costs are its only expenses, with no interest or tax expense.

**Sales and goods**

- Revenue from notebook sales: $1,000
- Cost of notebooks sold: $400
- Gross profit: **$600**

**Other expenses**

- Space hire: $200
- Advertising: $100
- Total other expenses: **$300**

**Final result**

- Gross profit less other expenses: **$300**

The shop's net profit is $300 under these assumptions. Calling the full $1,000 “profit” would ignore the costs of earning it.

## What the statement does not show

**Cash flow** means money actually moving into and out of a business. Revenue can relate to sales that have not yet been paid for. A **balance sheet** reports what a business owns and owes at a particular date. These are different questions from profit over a period. ([ASIC](https://www.asic.gov.au/regulatory-resources/financial-reporting-and-audit/users-of-financial-reports))

A profitable classroom shop could still be waiting for a customer to pay. The profit calculation alone cannot answer whether money is available for a bill today.

Records support the report. ASIC lists receipts, bank statements, loan documents and financial statements among company records. ([ASIC](https://www.asic.gov.au/for-business-and-companies/companies/company-building-blocks/company-record-keeping))

## Things to think about

- What period does the report cover?
- Are all relevant costs included, or does the final label exclude some?
- Would another report be needed to answer a question about available cash?

## Check your understanding

1. Which calculation gives gross profit?
   - a) Revenue minus cost of goods sold
   - b) Revenue plus every expense
   - c) The bank account number
2. True or false: A profit figure alone proves that every customer has paid.
3. What is the classroom shop's net profit, and why is it less than revenue?

## Answers

1. **a) Revenue minus cost of goods sold.** Other expenses have not yet been deducted.
2. **False.** Cash collection and reported revenue can occur at different times.
3. **$300.** The statement subtracts the goods sold and the other listed expenses.

## Glossary

- **Income statement:** A report of earnings and expenses over a period.
- **Revenue:** Money earned from sales.
- **Cost of goods sold:** Cost assigned to goods sold in the period.
- **Gross profit:** Revenue minus cost of goods sold.
- **Expenses:** Costs included in calculating the result.
- **Net profit:** Revenue after all included expenses.
- **Loss:** A result where expenses exceed revenue.
- **Cash flow:** Actual money moving in and out.
- **Balance sheet:** A report of what is owned and owed at a date.
