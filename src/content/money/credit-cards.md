# Credit cards

## What you will learn

- Explain the difference between borrowing and using money already held.
- Read the main amounts on a card statement.
- Explain how payments and new charges change a debt.

## A card that borrows money

A **credit card** allows purchases using money borrowed from a provider. **Debt** is money owed. A **credit limit** is the maximum the provider agrees to lend on the account. The limit is not the cardholder's savings or pay.

A **balance** is the amount owed at a particular time. **Available credit** is the amount still available within the limit, subject to the provider's rules. Having available credit is therefore different from having spare money.

Moneysmart explains that providers offer credit limits and that card costs differ. The limit and the cost of borrowing are separate features. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/credit-cards/choosing-a-credit-card))

## Read the statement carefully

A **statement** is a record of activity and amounts for a period. A card statement can bring together purchases, payments and charges. The **due date** is when a required payment must be made.

A **minimum repayment** is the smallest required payment for that statement period. It is not usually the same as the full balance. Moneysmart's calculator uses the card's minimum-payment settings to estimate how long repayment may take. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/credit-cards/credit-card-calculator))

These labels answer different questions. The balance describes what is owed. The minimum describes the required payment for now. The due date describes timing. Reading only one of these can leave the other parts of the obligation unclear.

## Interest-free days have conditions

**Interest** is a charge for borrowed money over time. **Interest-free days** are a period in which eligible purchases do not attract that charge if the card's conditions are met. Moneysmart states that these days depend on paying the full balance by the due date. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/credit-cards/choosing-a-credit-card))

A headline about interest-free days does not mean every transaction is free. A **fee** is another charge. Cards may have account or late-payment fees, among other charges. Their conditions need to be read separately from the interest-free description. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/credit-cards/choosing-a-credit-card))

The important distinction is between a conditional feature and an unconditional promise. A feature may be available only in particular circumstances. Naming it does not prove those circumstances apply.

## Why a debt can remain or grow

A card balance reflects amounts added and amounts paid. If new purchases and charges together exceed payments, the balance grows. If payments exceed those additions, it falls. This is an arithmetic relationship, not a prediction about a particular card.

Making a payment is therefore not enough information to decide whether debt fell. The amount added during the same period also matters.

Moneysmart explains that larger repayments can reduce repayment time and interest costs. That comparison needs consistent assumptions, including whether new spending occurs. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/credit-cards/pay-off-your-credit-card))

A classroom estimate that assumes no new purchases answers a different question from a household continuing to use the card. Both scenarios need clear labels.

## Worked example

*These numbers are not real. They only show the idea.*

An invented statement uses the charges below. No other transactions occur. This is not a real interest calculation or minimum-payment rule.

**Amount before payment**

- Starting balance: $500
- New purchases: $100
- Invented interest charge: $20
- Invented account fee: $10
- Total before payment: **$630**

**Payment and remaining debt**

- Payment made: $50
- Balance after payment: **$580**

The cardholder paid $50, but the balance is higher than the starting $500. The new purchases and charges added more than the payment removed.

## Things to think about

- Does a displayed amount mean money owned or money available to borrow?
- What conditions apply to interest-free days?
- Does a repayment estimate assume that spending stops?

## Check your understanding

1. What does a credit limit describe?
   - a) Money the cardholder has saved
   - b) The maximum agreed borrowing amount
   - c) A guaranteed monthly income
2. True or false: Paying the minimum always clears the whole balance.
3. Why did the balance rise in the worked example despite a payment?

## Answers

1. **b) The maximum agreed borrowing amount.** It is borrowed money, not savings.
2. **False.** A balance may remain after the minimum is paid.
3. **Purchases and charges exceeded the payment.** The account added $130 and removed $50.

## Glossary

- **Credit card:** A card used to borrow for purchases.
- **Debt:** Money owed.
- **Credit limit:** Maximum agreed borrowing amount.
- **Balance:** Amount owed at a particular time.
- **Available credit:** Unused borrowing capacity within the limit.
- **Statement:** Record of account activity and amounts.
- **Due date:** Required payment date.
- **Minimum repayment:** Smallest payment required for a statement period.
- **Interest:** Charge for borrowed money over time.
- **Interest-free days:** Conditional period without interest on eligible purchases.
- **Fee:** A separate charge.
