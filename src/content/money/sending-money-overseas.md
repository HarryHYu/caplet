# Sending money overseas

## What you will learn

- Explain how conversion and fees affect a transfer.
- Compare the sender's total cost and the receiver's amount.
- Recognise suspicious requests to send money.

## Two people, two amounts

An **international money transfer** moves money to someone in another country. The person sending it is the sender. The person receiving it is the receiver. Banks and specialist transfer businesses provide these services, with different methods and costs. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/banking/sending-money-overseas))

A **currency** is a country's or region's money, such as the Australian dollar. An **exchange rate** tells how much of one currency can be exchanged for another. The Reserve Bank of Australia, Australia's central bank, explains this relationship between currencies. ([RBA](https://www.rba.gov.au/education/resources/explainers/exchange-rates-and-the-australian-economy.html))

The sender and receiver may therefore see different numbers. That does not by itself mean money is missing. The amounts may use different currencies. Comparing them requires knowing the currency and the conversion used.

For a family sending support overseas, the useful questions are practical. How much leaves the sender's account? How much becomes available to the receiver? When and how can the receiver use it? Those are separate questions.

## Rates and charges

A **transfer fee** is a charge for sending money. A provider chooses its exchange rate and may also charge fees. Costs can depend on the amount, destination and method. Moneysmart includes receiver fees, conversion charges and whether the quoted rate is guaranteed among comparison points. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/banking/sending-money-overseas))

A **quote** is an offer showing the proposed price and conditions. One quote can include a lower fee but a less favourable conversion rate. Another can include a higher fee with a different conversion. The fee alone does not reveal the full result.

An exchange rate also needs a direction. “Foreign currency for one Australian dollar” is a different way of writing a rate from “Australian dollars for one unit of foreign currency”. Reading the units prevents a larger-looking number from being mistaken for a better result.

## Comparing like with like

One approach holds the sender's total spending constant and compares what each receiver would get. Another holds the receiver's amount constant and compares what the sender must pay.

Both approaches can be useful. Mixing them is the problem. A quote for a larger transfer cannot be judged only against the fee on a smaller transfer. Similarly, quotes obtained at different times might reflect different conditions.

A comparison sheet can have separate spaces for the sending currency, amount paid, fees, rate, receiving currency and amount received. This is a way to organise information, not a recommendation to use any particular provider.

Timing and access also matter. An amount is less useful to the receiver if it arrives after the intended expense. A collection method that requires travel has practical effects beyond the number on the quote.

## Worked example

*These numbers are not real. They only show the idea.*

A family asks two fictional providers for quotes delivering the same agreed foreign-currency amount. All figures below are the sender's Australian-dollar costs.

**Quote A**

- Australian dollars needed for conversion: $500
- Transfer fee: $20
- Total sender cost: **$520**

**Quote B**

- Australian dollars needed for conversion: $510
- Transfer fee: $0
- Total sender cost: **$510**

**Cost difference**

- Quote A total minus Quote B total: **$10**

Quote B is cheaper here, even though its conversion component costs more. A different pair of quotes could produce a different result. No real exchange rate is assumed, and the example does not predict future rates.

## Requests for money

A **scam** is a dishonest attempt to take money or personal information. A request can be false even if it claims to come from someone familiar. The identity of the requester and the legitimacy of the transfer service are separate matters.

Moneysmart explains that someone who has paid a scammer can report promptly to the bank or transfer provider and to Scamwatch, the Australian Government's scam-reporting service. It also warns about later offers to recover lost money. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/check-and-report-scams/what-to-do-if-youve-been-scammed))

Proof that money was sent does not prove the recipient was genuine. A transfer record answers the payment question; independent verification answers the identity question.

## Things to think about

- Does each quote deliver the same amount to the receiver?
- Is a request being checked through a contact method already known to be genuine?

## Check your understanding

1. Which is the most complete cost comparison?
   - a) Fees alone
   - b) Total sender cost for the same receiver amount
   - c) The provider's logo
2. True or false: A zero transfer fee always means the cheapest transfer.
3. Why must a rate's currency units be clear?

## Answers

1. **b) Total sender cost for the same receiver amount.** It accounts for conversion and fees.
2. **False.** The conversion rate also affects cost.
3. **They show which currency is being exchanged for which.** Otherwise the rate can be read in the wrong direction.

## Glossary

- **International money transfer:** Sending money to another country.
- **Currency:** A country's or region's money.
- **Exchange rate:** One currency's value in another.
- **Transfer fee:** A charge for sending money.
- **Quote:** A proposed price with conditions.
- **Scam:** A dishonest attempt to take money or information.
