# Keeping money records

## What you will learn

- Recognise common documents that explain money received and spent.
- Understand why different records answer different questions.
- Identify a record-keeping period for investments and the limits of that rule.

## Records explain what happened

A **receipt** records a purchase or payment. A **payslip** explains pay from an employer for a period. A **bank statement** lists activity in an account. These records can help connect a payment with its date, purpose and amount.

**Income** means money received. An **expense** is money spent on something. **Tax records** are documents used to support information about income, expenses and other matters relevant to tax. **Tax** is money paid to government under the law.

Each document has a different job. A bank statement can show a payment but may not explain everything bought. A receipt can describe the purchase, while a payslip explains how a payment from work was calculated. More than one document may be needed to understand an event.

## Investment records and timing

An **investment** uses money with the aim of future income or growth. A **capital gain** is a gain when an investment is sold for more than its relevant costs. A **capital loss** is the opposite.

Moneysmart explains that investment records include purchase and sale documents, payments received and expenses paid while owning the investment. Its guidance says to keep these records for five years after the income and capital gain or loss have been included in a **tax return**, the information submitted to report tax matters. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/how-to-invest/investing-and-tax))

This is not a rule to discard every money document five years after purchase. The starting event matters. A purchase document may still be needed while an investment is owned and when it is later sold.

## Making a record understandable

A useful record can be found and read when needed. A folder name that includes a date and topic can help distinguish similar documents. A note explaining a payment can connect it to the supporting document without changing the original.

**Personal information** is information that can identify someone. Money records may contain names, addresses and account details. Sharing a document also shares those details. A useful question is who needs access to which part of the record.

Record keeping is not only collecting files. It also means knowing what each file explains and which question remains unanswered.

## Glossary

- **Receipt:** A record of a purchase or payment.
- **Payslip:** A document explaining pay from an employer.
- **Bank statement:** A record of account activity.
- **Income:** Money received.
- **Expense:** Money spent on something.
- **Tax records:** Documents supporting information about tax matters.
- **Tax:** Money paid to government under the law.
- **Investment:** Money used for possible future income or growth.
- **Capital gain or loss:** The difference between sale proceeds and relevant investment costs.
- **Tax return:** Information submitted to report tax matters.
- **Personal information:** Information that can identify someone.
