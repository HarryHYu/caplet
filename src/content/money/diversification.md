# Diversification: spreading investment risk

## What you will learn

- Explain why the contents of a collection matter.
- Recognise when apparently different holdings depend on similar conditions.
- Describe what spreading money can and cannot achieve.

## What diversification means

**Investing** means using money with the aim of future income or growth in value. **Risk** means the chance of a worse result, including losing money. **Diversification** means spreading investment money across different holdings to reduce dependence on any one of them. A **portfolio** is the collection of investments someone owns.

Spreading across businesses, industries, countries and types of investments can reduce the effect of one poor result. It cannot promise that the total will never fall. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/how-to-invest/diversification))

The main question is not simply “How many things are there?” It is also “How similar are the things?” Different labels do not always mean different sources of risk.

## Different types and shared risks

An **asset** is something owned that has value. An **asset class** is a group of similar investments. Common classes include cash, shares, bonds and property. A **share** is part ownership of a company. A **bond** is a loan to a government or business under stated terms. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/how-to-invest/diversification))

**Concentration** means a large part of a portfolio depends on one holding or a narrow group. A collection of company shares might still depend heavily on one industry. A broader collection can include businesses affected by different conditions. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/how-to-invest/diversification))

For a classroom discussion, imagine a list of business names with the industry hidden. At first, the list looks varied. Revealing that every business serves the same kind of customer changes what the list means. Counting names was useful, but it did not answer the whole question.

## Looking inside a fund

A **managed fund** combines money from many people under an investment plan. A **fund manager** manages that money. A fund may own one type of asset or several types. Fund investors own a part of the fund, rather than directly owning every investment it holds. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/managed-funds-and-etfs/what-is-a-managed-fund))

This makes the list of holdings important. Two differently named funds could contain some of the same investments. In that case, counting the funds alone would miss the repeated holdings.

A useful way to understand this is to write a list of what is actually owned. The same item appearing twice is still the same item. A second wrapper or account name does not change that fact.

## Worked example

*These numbers are not real. They only show the idea.*

Two fictional portfolios begin with $1,000. In this imagined outcome, Company A loses half its value. Company B stays unchanged. Fees, tax and payments to owners are left out.

**Portfolio concentrated in Company A**

- Company A at the start: $1,000
- Company A at the end: $500
- Total ending value: **$500**

**Portfolio split between two companies**

- Company A at the start: $500
- Company B at the start: $500
- Company A at the end: $250
- Company B at the end: $500
- Total ending value: **$750**

The split portfolio loses less in this particular outcome because less money depends on Company A. It still loses money. Two companies alone are not a claim of broad diversification.

## What this example cannot prove

The numbers were chosen to isolate one idea. They do not estimate the chance that either company falls. They also do not show which portfolio would earn more in another outcome.

If both companies had the same fall, the comparison would look different. If Company A rose strongly, putting more money there would produce a larger gain in that imagined case. Those possibilities explain why a good outcome after the event does not prove that the original choice involved little risk.

Diversification changes dependence. It does not create a fixed ending value. Moneysmart explains that different investments can perform differently at different times. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/how-to-invest/diversification))

## Things to think about

- What shared event might affect several holdings together?
- Does the list show the underlying investments, or only account and fund names?

## Check your understanding

1. What does diversification aim to reduce?
   - a) The need to understand holdings
   - b) Dependence on one investment or narrow group
   - c) Every possible loss
2. True or false: Several funds always mean there are no repeated underlying investments.
3. Why did the split portfolio lose less in the worked example?

## Answers

1. **b) Dependence on one investment or narrow group.** Spreading holdings can limit the effect of one poor result.
2. **False.** Different funds can contain the same holdings.
3. **Less money was in Company A, and Company B stayed unchanged.** That assumed difference caused the smaller loss.

## Glossary

- **Investing:** Using money for possible future income or growth.
- **Risk:** The chance of a worse result, including loss.
- **Diversification:** Spreading holdings to reduce dependence on a narrow group.
- **Portfolio:** A collection of investments.
- **Asset:** Something owned that has value.
- **Asset class:** A group of similar investments.
- **Share:** Part ownership of a company.
- **Bond:** A loan to a government or business under stated terms.
- **Concentration:** Heavy dependence on one holding or narrow group.
- **Managed fund:** Money from many people invested under a shared plan.
- **Fund manager:** The person or business managing a fund's investments.
