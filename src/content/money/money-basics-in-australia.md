# Money basics in Australia

## What you will learn

- Recognise the main systems used to manage money.
- Match common questions to the right organisation.
- Find official information and free explanations.

## Money for different purposes

Moving to another country can mean learning new words for familiar tasks. Receiving pay, paying a bill and saving for later are different tasks. Knowing which system handles each task makes forms and messages easier to understand.

A **bank account** is a record of money held with a bank. A **transaction account** is used for everyday payments and receiving wages, which are payments for work. Moneysmart describes transaction accounts as everyday accounts. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/banking/transaction-accounts-and-debit-cards))

An account is not the same thing as the card used to access it. A number on a card and a number identifying an account serve different purposes. When reading a form, the name of the requested detail matters as much as the number itself. Copying a number without understanding the label can cause confusion.

## Tax and money for later life

**Tax** is money collected by government under its rules. The ATO (Australian Taxation Office) is the government agency that administers Australian tax. A **tax file number (TFN)** is the personal number the ATO assigns for tax administration. Employers and banks are among the organisations that use it for tax purposes. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/glossary/tax-file-number-tfn))

**Superannuation**, often called super, is money saved for retirement, the stage of life when someone stops working. A **super fund** is an organisation that manages and invests this money. Eligible workers receive payments into super from their employers. Rules control when super can be accessed. It is not an everyday spending account. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/how-super-works/superannuation))

These systems can appear together in work documents. However, a tax number identifies a person; it does not store money. A bank account holds money for banking. Super holds money for a different purpose. Learning these distinctions helps a family understand where a payment has gone.

## Borrowing and personal information

**Credit** means receiving money or something now under an agreement to pay later. A **credit history** is a record of a person's past use of credit. A **credit report** contains information about credit accounts and repayments. Businesses that lend money use this information when assessing applications. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/managing-debt/credit-scores-and-credit-reports))

This is different from having cash available. A person might have savings and also have an unpaid borrowing agreement. Knowing one fact does not establish the other.

A **scam** is a dishonest attempt to take money or personal information. Government impersonation means pretending to represent a government organisation. Services Australia, the government agency delivering payments and public services, describes fake messages that direct people to false sign-in pages, and calls that use threats or promised payments. ([Services Australia](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/types-scams?context=60271))

A familiar logo is only an image. It does not prove who sent a message. Someone unfamiliar with local agencies may find official-looking language hard to judge. That is a reason to use independent information, not a reason to feel embarrassed.

## Who explains the systems?

ASIC (Australian Securities and Investments Commission) is Australia's corporate, markets and financial services regulator. Its Moneysmart website provides free money information. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/about-us))

Services Australia's work includes Centrelink, Medicare and Child Support. It is separate from a bank and from the ATO. ([Services Australia](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/about-us))

Its **Financial Information Service** provides free education about money matters, with information available in different languages. Education explains a system; it does not choose a product for a family. ([Services Australia](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/financial-information-service))

## Worked example

*These numbers are not real. They only show the idea.*

A household records money received and everyday spending. This example does not calculate tax or super.

**Money received**

- Money available after work deductions: $1,000
- Total received: **$1,000**

**Everyday spending**

- Food: $300
- Transport: $100
- Total spending: **$400**

**Money remaining**

- Received less spending: **$600**

The record shows everyday money only. A separate super account would not be added to the amount available for groceries.

## Things to think about

- Which words on a money form are unfamiliar?
- Is the question about banking, tax, retirement money or government services?

## Check your understanding

1. Which item identifies a person for tax administration?
   - a) A shopping receipt
   - b) A TFN
   - c) A super balance
2. True or false: Super is an everyday spending account.
3. Name one official source of free money education.

## Answers

1. **b) A TFN.** It is used for tax administration.
2. **False.** Super is for retirement and access is restricted.
3. **Moneysmart or the Financial Information Service.** Both provide free education.

## Glossary

- **Bank account:** A record of money held with a bank.
- **Transaction account:** An account for everyday banking.
- **Tax:** Money collected by government under its rules.
- **Tax file number (TFN):** A personal number used for tax administration.
- **Superannuation:** Money saved for retirement.
- **Super fund:** An organisation managing super money.
- **Credit:** An arrangement to receive something now and pay later.
- **Credit history:** A record of someone's use of credit.
- **Credit report:** Information about credit accounts and repayments.
- **Scam:** A dishonest attempt to take money or information.
