# Spotting common scams

## What you will learn

- Recognise pressure and requests for private information.
- Understand false government, work and investment offers.
- Identify official reporting and support information.

## Look at the request, not only the appearance

A **scam** is a dishonest attempt to take money or information. Scammers may make unexpected contact, promise something valuable, then ask for payment or private details. Their message may look friendly, helpful or official. ([Moneysmart factsheet](https://files.moneysmart.gov.au/media/3czjydos/scams.pdf))

A useful reading task is to separate three parts of a message: who the sender claims to be, what they promise, and what they want. A claim about identity is not evidence of identity. A promise is not proof that money or a job exists.

People new to Australia may be learning unfamiliar agency names and procedures. It can be hard to know whether a request is normal. Language difficulty is not a lack of intelligence. A clear explanation takes time, while pressure makes careful reading harder.

A message can contain a real name but a false instruction. Knowing the name of a bank or government office does not verify the sender. The request itself still needs an explanation.

## Government impersonation

**Impersonation** means pretending to be someone else. Services Australia, the government agency delivering payments and public services, describes messages claiming urgent action is needed on a government account. Links may lead to fake sign-in pages. Telephone scammers may demand payment or promise money while claiming to represent the agency. ([Services Australia](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/types-scams?context=60271))

**myGov** is an account for accessing linked government services. Sharing its sign-in details with a false website can give another person access to real information. Services Australia explains that this can happen without the government system itself being broken into. ([Services Australia](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/scams-and-identity-theft?context=60271))

Someone who is unsure of a caller can use an independently found contact channel to check the claim. The agency's official guidance describes ending an uncertain call and using the number on its website. ([Services Australia](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/types-scams?context=60271))

## Investment and job offers

An **investment** uses money with the aim of receiving a future return. A **return** is a gain or loss from that investment. Investment scammers can use convincing websites and pretend to represent a genuine business or well-known person. Attractive presentation does not establish that the offered product exists. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/financial-scams/investment-scams))

A **job scam** uses a false work opportunity to seek money or information. Moneysmart includes high-paying job promises among scam approaches. It also describes demands for money, computer access and account details. ([Moneysmart factsheet](https://files.moneysmart.gov.au/media/3czjydos/scams.pdf))

A learner can ask what work is actually described, who would employ the worker, and why payment is being requested. These questions do not prove an offer is genuine. They help identify what still has no evidence.

## Reporting and getting support

For scams involving its services or myGov, Services Australia accepts reports at reportascam@servicesaustralia.gov.au. Its Scams and Identity Theft Helpdesk provides support, including interpreter arrangements. ([Services Australia](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/scams-and-identity-theft?context=60271))

Moneysmart describes contacting the bank or payment provider promptly, reporting to Scamwatch, and being alert to later offers to recover money. **Scamwatch** is the Australian Government's scam-reporting service. A report does not guarantee that money will be recovered. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/check-and-report-scams/what-to-do-if-youve-been-scammed))

A record of what happened can separate facts from guesses: the message received, details shared and payment made. Feeling upset does not change the right to seek help.

## Worked example

*These numbers are not real. They only show the idea.*

A fictional caller promises work but demands charges first. The person records the requests without paying.

**Requested payments**

- Claimed application charge: $100
- Claimed account release charge: $200
- Total requested: **$300**

**Actual outcome**

- Money sent: $0
- Pay received: $0
- Total money exchanged: **$0**

A promise of future pay is not money received. The requested charges are facts to investigate, not proof that a job exists.

## Things to think about

- Does the message explain a real purpose for the information requested?
- Can the sender's identity be checked without using their link?

## Check your understanding

1. Which is a warning sign?
   - a) Time to read information
   - b) Clear written conditions
   - c) Pressure to pay immediately
2. True or false: A real organisation's logo proves a message is genuine.
3. Name one place described in this lesson for reporting a scam.

## Answers

1. **c) Pressure to pay immediately.** It can discourage careful checking.
2. **False.** Images and names can be copied.
3. **Scamwatch, or Services Australia for a scam involving its services.** Reporting and recovering money are different outcomes.

## Glossary

- **Scam:** A dishonest attempt to take money or information.
- **Impersonation:** Pretending to be someone else.
- **myGov:** An account for linked government services.
- **Investment:** Money used to seek a future return.
- **Return:** An investment gain or loss.
- **Job scam:** A false work offer seeking money or information.
