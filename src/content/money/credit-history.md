# Credit history and credit reports

## What you will learn

- Distinguish a report from a score.
- Understand why records of repayments matter.
- Recognise free report access and error correction.

## The record and the number

**Credit** is an agreement to receive money or something else now and pay later. A **lender** provides money under such an agreement. A **repayment** is money paid back.

A **credit report** records credit-related information about a person. A **credit score** is a number calculated using report information. A **credit history** is the person's past record of using credit. Lenders consider these records when deciding whether to lend. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/managing-debt/credit-scores-and-credit-reports))

A report and a score are different tools. The report contains details; the score summarises information using a calculation. A single number cannot explain every entry. Reading the details helps someone understand what the number refers to.

A report is also different from a **budget**, a plan for money coming in and going out. One describes recorded credit activity. The other helps organise expected spending. A credit score is not a measure of a person's character.

## What can affect the record?

Moneysmart identifies borrowing amounts, applications for credit and whether repayments arrive on time as factors used in credit scores. Reports contain details about accounts and repayment history. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/managing-debt/credit-scores-and-credit-reports))

**Repayment history** describes payments in relation to what was due and when. This explains why dates matter alongside amounts. A list showing only the total paid may leave out whether individual payments were made when expected.

Borrowing again is not the same as showing that an earlier amount was repaid. Likewise, closing an account is not proof that every entry about it was wrong. These are different events and need different evidence.

There is no score calculation in this lesson. An arithmetic example can show a remaining amount owed, but it cannot predict exactly how a reporting body's scoring method will respond.

## Getting a report

A **credit reporting body** collects credit information and produces reports. A person can obtain a free credit report every three months. Different reporting bodies may hold different information. Moneysmart provides links for requesting reports. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/managing-debt/credit-scores-and-credit-reports))

The reason for looking at a report is to understand its contents. A name, address, account or amount that seems unfamiliar deserves a clear explanation. A report about someone else would not answer questions about your own records.

When reviewing a record, it helps to separate two questions: Is the information accurate? And what does that information mean? An accurate item can be unwelcome. An inaccurate item needs correction. The two situations are not interchangeable.

## Correcting mistakes

Errors can include a duplicated borrowing amount, incorrect personal details or an account created through identity misuse. A person can ask the reporting body or lender to help correct an error for free. Correct negative information cannot simply be removed by paying a credit repair business. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/managing-debt/credit-repair))

**Identity theft** means someone uses another person's information to pretend to be them. Moneysmart identifies unexpected account-related contact and unfamiliar charges as possible warning signs. ([Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/online-safety/identity-theft))

A request to correct an error can explain which entry is disputed and why. Dates, account labels and payment records make that explanation easier to follow. This is different from sending a general complaint that the score seems too low.

## Worked example

*These numbers are not real. They only show the idea.*

An adult compares a practice borrowing record with their payment record. Interest and fees are excluded.

**Borrowing**

- Original amount owed: $1,000
- Total borrowing: **$1,000**

**Repayments recorded**

- First payment: $200
- Second payment: $300
- Total repayments: **$500**

**Amount still owed**

- Original amount less repayments: **$500**

If the record omitted the second payment, the remaining amount would appear too high. The payment evidence could explain the error. This calculation does not estimate a credit score or show how quickly any correction changes it.

## Things to think about

- Does an unfamiliar entry belong to you?
- Which record could show that a disputed payment was made?

## Check your understanding

1. Which contains detailed credit information?
   - a) A shopping list
   - b) A credit report
   - c) A budget heading
2. True or false: Paying a business guarantees removal of accurate negative information.
3. Why are payment dates useful as well as payment amounts?

## Answers

1. **b) A credit report.** A score is a numerical summary.
2. **False.** Accurate negative information cannot simply be removed for a fee.
3. **They show whether payments matched due dates.** Totals alone do not show timing.

## Glossary

- **Credit:** Receiving something now under an agreement to pay later.
- **Lender:** A provider of borrowed money.
- **Repayment:** Money paid back.
- **Credit report:** A record of credit information.
- **Credit score:** A number based on report information.
- **Credit history:** Past use of credit.
- **Budget:** A plan for money received and spent.
- **Repayment history:** A record of payments and their timing.
- **Credit reporting body:** An organisation producing credit reports.
- **Identity theft:** Pretending to be someone using their information.
